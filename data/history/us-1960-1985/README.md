# United States, 1960–1985

Frozen official BEA, BLS and Federal Reserve data distributed by FRED. The CSV files retain 1959 for lag construction, 1960–1969 for calibration, and 1970–1985 for evaluation. Source URLs, units, retrieval date and SHA-256 hashes are in `manifest.json`. The first retrieval used FRED's date/value table pages because its CSV endpoint timed out. These are revised observations, not vintage data available to historical decision makers.

`npm run evaluate:history` verifies all data hashes and writes `docs/validation/us-1960-1985-v1.json`. No network or secret API key is needed. `python3 scripts/fetch-us-history.py` explicitly refreshes the data from FRED when a new vintage is intended; it must not run as part of tests. A new vintage changes the experiment and should get a new report.

GDP is a quarterly seasonally adjusted annual-rate level, not a quantity to sum across quarters. Monthly CPI, unemployment, labor force, policy rates and domestic crude-petroleum PPI become three-month arithmetic means. GDP growth is `400 × log(GDP[t]/GDP[t−1])`; inflation is `100 × log(CPI[t]/CPI[t−4])`. Rates become fractions internally. Labor force converts thousands to people. There is no interpolation or imputation of missing observations; missing quarters fail ingestion.

The forecast origin is 1969Q4. GDP, CPI, unemployment and labor force after that date are scoring data only. Conditional experiments may replay the recorded federal funds rate and domestic oil-price path. Their counterfactuals hold energy at its origin price or substitute a modern inertial policy rule. Recorded policy is endogenous to historical conditions; domestic oil prices include demand, regulation and supply effects. These paths are not identified exogenous shocks, and their paired differences are model experiments rather than causal estimates of the historical embargo or Volcker policy.

The benchmark is a quarterly aggregate IS/Phillips/search model sharing the world's matching and policy-rate functions. It does not replay the spatial simulation, reconstruct US geography or firms, validate its transaction ledger, or estimate wage bargaining. The world still lacks an explicit petroleum sector and a historical monetary-regime transition. Fixed 1960s potential-output and labor-supply trends also omit the later productivity slowdown and participation changes. The first report fails the declared baseline comparison; do not describe it as historical validation of Uchrony.
