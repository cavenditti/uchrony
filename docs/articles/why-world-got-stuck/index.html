Carlo Venditti · Uchrony

Inside a simulated world

Why my simulated
world got stuck

A story about money that stopped moving, workshops that ran out of materials, and governments that missed their meetings.

Illustrated draft · experiments from 27 October 2026

Imagine a town where shops have money, people need jobs, and there is still room to build. Yet the town slowly gets poorer. Workshops stand idle. New settlements stop appearing. Governments barely act.

That was happening in some long runs of Uchrony, a simulation of a world with people, businesses, trade and competing states. Its calendar kept advancing. Much of its economy had stopped moving.

The investigation became a lesson in how small rules can produce very large consequences. A shop’s savings rule can weaken demand. A shopping rule can empty a factory’s shelves. A calendar shortcut can silence a government for centuries.

You do not need to know economics or programming to follow what happened. Start with a shop till.

01 / Money

The money was there.
It had stopped coming back.

People bought goods from merchants. The merchants accumulated cash, including money they did not need for their day-to-day business. Meanwhile, households’ ability to keep buying weakened.

Think of a board game where one player keeps collecting money but rarely spends or distributes it. Counting all the money on the board misses the problem: who can use it next?

The revised rule gives surplus cash a way back into circulation. Merchants first keep enough for six months of observed operating costs, plus money already committed to construction. A limited share of the excess can then go to their actual owners.

This is an ownership payment, so it does not go equally to everyone. It also creates no new money. It changes where existing money can go.

02 / Food

A richer person does not need endless dinners

Getting money moving revealed a second problem. The old shopping rule let households order ever more food as their spending power grew. Wealthy households could demand enormous quantities while everyone competed for a limited supply.

In the first trial, circulation improved and measured unemployment was low. But the population fell by 44% in just twenty years. That trial was rejected.

The fix gives food demand a ceiling of 1.5 subsistence baskets. A basket is the model’s unit for basic food needs; the ceiling means one and a half times that amount. Beyond it, extra spending shifts toward other goods.

A ceiling on demand does not guarantee dinner. Households still need money, and the food still has to exist. But it prevents unlimited orders from swallowing the market.

A low unemployment number is not a success if the population is starving.

03 / Production

You cannot make tools from an empty shelf

Some goods are used twice: people buy them for everyday life, and businesses use them to make something else. Economists call those business supplies intermediate inputs. Think of timber going into a workshop and tools coming out.

Household purchases could clear out all those supplies. At the next hiring decision, workshops found nothing to work with. They hired no industrial workers, and production stayed stuck.

Wholesalers can now reserve up to half of their stock against expected business orders. It is a limited reserve tied to expected need, not an automatic fifty-fifty split. Food is excluded.

The point is to keep a production chain alive. A pile of timber can satisfy a purchase today, or help a workshop produce tomorrow. The simulation needs to account for both uses.

04 / Building

A promising project still needs a turn in the queue

A business might have a worthwhile expansion in mind and enough cash to pay for it. But productive investment used to be considered after public hiring, household shopping and housing construction. By then, the workers or materials could be gone.

Funded productive projects now get an earlier opportunity. They must still offer a return above their financing costs and find real workers and materials. Giving a project an earlier turn does not give it a free factory.

There was also a subtler timing mistake. Imagine judging a shop’s prospects before today’s customers have arrived. Looking only at cash already received makes the shop seem weaker than it is.

The latest correction also considers expected retail sales, bounded by stock the firm already holds. That estimate can help answer “is this project worthwhile?” It cannot answer “do we have the cash to pay?” Expected revenue is never spendable funding.

05 / Prices

A price tag can outlive the economy it was written for

Over decades, wages and prices could fall. Some costs for recruiting soldiers and founding settlements stayed fixed in the original currency amounts. That made them much harder to afford in terms of work.

Suppose a fee is 600 coins when a monthly wage is 100. It represents six months of wages. If the wage later falls to 20, the same fee represents thirty months. The number on the price tag has not changed; the burden has.

The revised obligations follow domestic wages: six months per pioneer for an automatic settlement, and six months per recruit for training. Food, weapons, materials and people remain separate physical requirements.

Settlement searches were repaired too. Governments now exclude occupied sites before taking a limited shortlist, so they can discover available places beyond the same blocked candidates.

06 / Government

Fast-forward was skipping the meetings

Different governments were scheduled to review decisions in different months. At high speed, the simulation advanced six months at a time. The old check asked whether a meeting was due on the month it landed on.

Imagine checking your calendar only on Sunday and concluding that you had no appointments all week. In the simulation, that shortcut permanently skipped two thirds of cabinets.

The new check asks whether the time interval crossed a scheduled review date. Governments get their opportunity to act. Their ambitions, domestic support, supplies, stability, funds and military readiness still determine what they do.

Other repairs made that opportunity usable. Half of military funding is now retained for operations, and armies buy supplies and replacements before household consumption. Readiness follows current wages, and a country can form a small initial force.

Native diplomacy also had an accidental dependency on records normally created by optional AI agents. Removing that dependency required restoring the whole diplomatic path: proposals, consent, trade, alliances and peace. An early offensive-only trial was stopped when its missing peace path became clear.

Autonomous governments can now negotiate through the normal rules. Human-controlled governments still require the human’s agreement. And a functioning government is allowed to remain peaceful.

07 / Experiments

Repair the machinery. Then adjust the dials.

A broken rule and a badly chosen number are different problems. Once the initial economic repairs were in place, a small search tried different settings for four decisions: how quickly merchants distribute excess cash, how unattractive work is in the household’s trade-off, how much firm cash is available for investment, and how quickly capital adjusts.

Think of testing seven versions of a recipe. Each candidate starts from the same saved world, changes a few bounded quantities, and runs for twenty simulated years. The simulator’s rules stay fixed within that search.

The score favored population and total real output growth, gave a smaller reward to territory growth, and penalized unemployment and food shortfalls. Here, real output measures production with price changes accounted for. Wars earned no reward.

All seven candidates passed the stated safeguards. The selected one ended its short test with 107,217 people, up 16.37%, and 4.80% average unemployment.

This was an offline parameter search. No language model was being fine-tuned, and no AI was learning to govern during play. The score expressed design preferences; it was not a scientific definition of human welfare.

The short search also preceded the final inventory and diplomacy repairs. Its result was a promising setting to test further, not proof that the complete model was solved.

08 / Results

Let the same world run for another two hundred years

The main recovery comparison began from a saved world called Gino, at year 200, with 92,136 people. One branch used the previous policy. Another used the revised rules and selected economic settings.

The revised branch grew to 233,872 people; the old branch shrank to 61,755. Average unemployment fell from 14.38% to 4.56%, about a 68% reduction. Claimed provinces rose to 72 rather than 26. The revised branch recorded two wars and two peace agreements; the control recorded neither.

These are results for the tested versions of this world. This comparison predates the final retail-return correction described below.

One useful diagnostic isolated the earlier economic changes. Circulation and food satiation alone produced a recovery to 219,547 people, without wars or new expansion in that interval. That helped identify an economic problem, but did not validate the later military and inventory changes.

A good average can hide a bad ending

Food coverage describes how much of the model’s food requirement is met. It is not the percentage of people who are completely well-fed. The reported average combines population-weighted regional coverage over time.

The revised recovery branch averaged 90.91% food coverage, but ended at 78.51%. Another long Gino run ended with 9.42% unemployment and 77.29% food coverage. Late shortages and employment cycles remain unresolved.

More activity has costs

Restoring conflict did not automatically improve life. In a matched year-70-to-300 comparison, the branch with repaired diplomacy had nine wars and nine armistices, and ended with 190,769 people. The branch without that repair remained warless and ended with 224,789.

A separate world, Maritime, had no wars during fifty additional years. That is a valid result. Requiring a certain number of wars would hide the differences between worlds.

09 / The latest change

The honest result has arrows in both directions

The final investment correction received its own comparison. Both branches started from the same year-400 save and ran to year 450, using the same selected economic settings. The only simulation difference was the bounded retail-return estimate.

The latest model ended with more people and better average food coverage. It also had worse average unemployment, much worse unemployment at the endpoint, and lower output per person at that endpoint. It reached 98 claimed provinces rather than 96; both branches had one war and one peace agreement.

The correction removes an unfair timing penalty from investment decisions. This particular continuation does not show an across-the-board welfare improvement. One pair of runs cannot establish its general effect.

10 / Trust

A world should survive being saved

A convincing-looking history is only part of the job. The simulation also needs to account for its people, money, goods and banks. And saving a world must preserve what happens next.

Stress tests found real failures. One run crashed when territory became unclaimed. Another hit a census-record limit that was too low for a large, mixed population. Those were repaired, and the failed runs remain recorded as failures.

The dense Borderlands world eventually reached year 100 with 22.4 million people, eight surviving states, 31 war starts and 30 peace agreements. Its saved world survived, but the final comparison exhausted the test process’s 4 GiB memory allowance.

After reducing unnecessary copies, a separate recovery test used a 6 GiB allowance, restored that save, and continued through year 101. Accounting and exact save continuation passed. That validates the recovered world; it does not establish how much memory a browser needs. The reported 73 province ownership changes also include peaceful transfers, so they are not 73 military conquests.

A living world depends on the connections

The strongest lesson from these experiments is how much depends on the links between systems. Money needs a way back to buyers. Producers need inputs before they can hire. Investment needs a fair opportunity to use resources. Governments need to notice that their meeting date has passed.

Repairing those connections brought growth, settlement and political activity back to worlds that had stalled. It also exposed problems the next experiments need to tackle: recurring shortages, uneven employment, and trade-offs that a single headline number cannot describe.

The next useful search should span several mature worlds and judge sustained employment alongside households’ access to food and materials. New seeds should test whether those settings travel well. Physical resources and diplomatic consent should remain constraints.

A simulation becomes more credible when its successes can be explained, its failures remain visible, and its next hundred years are still worth investigating.
